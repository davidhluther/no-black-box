# Glossary

Every technical or official term used in this kit, in plain words. Terms are in alphabetical order.

**Adopted minutes.** The official written record of what a public body did at a meeting, adopted by a vote at a later meeting. When a note, a memo, or a transcript disagrees with the adopted minutes, the minutes win.

**Agenda packet.** The bundle of documents a public body posts before a meeting, usually a PDF. It holds the agenda plus the reports, maps, draft ordinances, and letters the members will read. Think of it as the homework pile the members get.

**AI model.** A computer program that writes and answers in ordinary language, such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. It predicts likely words, so it can sound sure while being wrong.

**American Community Survey.** A yearly survey by the U.S. Census Bureau that asks a sample of households about income, rent, age, jobs, and housing. Its results are estimates, which is why each comes with a margin of error. Often shortened to ACS.

**API.** A way for two programs to talk, like a waiter carrying orders between you and the kitchen. The Census Bureau's API lets a script ask for numbers without anyone clicking through web pages.

**API key.** A long code, like a library card number, that tells a service who is asking. Keep it private and never paste it in a chat or a screenshot.

**Apps Script.** Google's free tool for adding small programs to a Google Sheet. In this kit it adds the check-up button and an optional date highlighter.

**Captions.** The words on screen at the bottom of a video, shown with the time they were spoken. Many sites make them automatically from the sound.

**Check-up.** A button in the kit's Google Sheet that tests whether the sheet is set up correctly and prints a support code.

**Claude Code.** A program from Anthropic that you run on your own computer and that can read and write files in a folder you point it to. It is the full version's home.

**Clerk.** The staff member who keeps a town's, county's, or board's official records, such as agendas, minutes, and sign-up lists. The clerk is usually the right person to ask about meeting rules.

**Config file.** A small text file that holds your own details (your name, state, the board you follow) so you type them once. In this kit it is `config.json`, copied from `config.example.json`.

**CSV.** A plain table saved as text, with commas between the columns. Spreadsheets open it and can save to it. The name stands for comma-separated values.

**Custom GPT.** A version of ChatGPT that you set up once with your own instructions and files, so you do not paste them every time. A ChatGPT Project does a similar job.

**Environment variable.** A named setting that lives in your computer's memory for one terminal session, rather than in a file. The full version keeps the Census key in one, so the key never sits in a document.

**Exemption.** A reason in the law that lets a government office keep some records back. Personal medical details and active legal advice are common examples. Which exemptions apply depends on your state.

**FIPS code.** A short number the government uses to name a state (two digits) or a county (three more digits). Using the code avoids mix-ups between places with similar names.

**Five-year estimate.** An American Community Survey result built from five years of survey responses pooled together, such as 2020 to 2024. It is steadier than a one-year result, which is why it is the right choice for small counties.

**Legislative.** A decision that sets a general rule or policy, such as changing a zoning map or passing an ordinance. Decision-makers may usually talk with residents about it beforehand, but check your own rules.

**Margin of error.** The plus-or-minus amount that goes with an estimate (a number worked out from a sample instead of a full count). An income of $52,000 plus or minus $3,000 means the true figure is probably between $49,000 and $55,000.

**Ordinance.** A local law passed by a town, city, or county governing board.

**Prompt.** The message you give an AI model. A good prompt says who you are, what you want, what rules to follow, and what material to use.

**Public comment.** The time set aside at a public meeting for residents to speak or submit writing to the body. Rules about sign-up and time limits vary.

**Public-records request.** A written ask for copies of records a government office already holds. The law that covers it differs by state and may be called an open-records, right-to-know, or public-records law.

**Python.** A programming language. Some of the kit's full version scripts are written in it. You do not write Python to use them.

**Quasi-judicial.** A decision about one specific property or person that applies set standards to evidence, much like a judge. Different rules usually apply to what members may hear outside the hearing.

**Redaction.** Blacking out part of a record, usually to protect private details, before releasing the rest.

**Script.** A small program in a text file that does a job when you run it. The kit's scripts are short, readable, and only read public data.

**Skill.** A folder of written instructions (and sometimes a script) that an AI program can load when you ask for a certain job. A skill tells the AI model how to do the job well.

**Step ID.** The code printed at the start of every numbered step in the kit, such as CV-FR-01-3. Tell it to whoever helps you.

**Support code.** The short code the check-up prints, such as CV-FR-01-2-E01-v0.1.0-BBZ. It tells a helper which step, which known problem, which kit version, and a check value.

**Terminal.** A window on your computer where you type instructions instead of clicking. On a Mac it is the Terminal app, and on Windows it is PowerShell. Only the full version uses it.

**Timestamp.** The hours, minutes, and seconds mark showing when in a video something was said, such as 00:42:10.

**Transcript.** The words of a meeting written out in order. In this kit, a transcript made from captions is machine-made and unofficial.

**VTT file.** A caption file that stores each line of speech with its start and end time. The name is short for WebVTT.

**yt-dlp.** A free, open-source program you run in a terminal that can fetch the caption files (and, if asked, video) from many video sites. The kit uses it for captions only.
