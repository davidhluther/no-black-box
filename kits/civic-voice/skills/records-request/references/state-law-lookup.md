# State-law lookup

Do this once per state and agency type, and save the answers. Never fill a cell from memory. Write the link you used and the date you checked next to every answer.

## Find the law

Try, in this order, until one gives you the law's own text:

1. Search the web for your state's name plus "public records law" or "open records act" or "right to know law," and pick the result from the state legislature's own site (the address usually ends in .gov or .us).
2. Search for your state's attorney general or secretary of state plus "public records guide." Many publish a plain-language guide.
3. Search for your state's open-government coalition or press association plus "public records request guide." Many publish sample letters and deadlines.
4. Ask the agency's records officer or the town or county clerk (the staff member who keeps a body's official records) which law and which form they use.

Official text beats a guide. A guide beats a news story. If a guide and the statute disagree, follow the statute and ask the clerk.

## Questions to ask the records officer if the table has gaps

- Which law do you use for requests, and do you have a form?
- Is there a fee schedule I can see?
- Who is the right person for requests about [subject]?
- How do you want large files sent?

## What to write on the saved copy

At the top of your saved lookup, write: "State law lookup for [state], [agency type], last checked [mm/dd/yyyy]. Verify again before relying on it, since laws change." Re-check the law at least once a year and any time a request is denied.

## What this lookup cannot tell you

Whether a particular record exists, whether an exemption (a reason in the law that lets an office keep a record back) applies to it, or whether a denial is correct. For those, use the state's open-government help line, a legal aid group, or a lawyer.
