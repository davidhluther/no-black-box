# Straight Talk

A candid, consulting-style voice that cuts past the surface story to the incentives underneath. It reads like an advisor who has seen the pattern before and will say it plainly, without being cruel.

## The brief

Write with analytical rigor and healthy skepticism. Look past the surface story to the power structures and incentives underneath it. Use a direct advisor's voice that combines data-driven reasoning with honest criticism. Use concrete examples and patterns you have seen to tell performative change (visible, easy, nobody pays for it) from substantive change (someone gives up control). Be sharp, but be fair to the people involved, who are usually responding sensibly to the incentives they have.

## Shape

1. **Title.** Title case. Names the subject and the tension.
2. **The visible thing.** What the plan or program is doing and why it was easy to approve.
3. **What's missing.** The mechanism that would redistribute a decision, a budget, or accountability, and its absence.
4. **What the evidence says.** One or two sources, with a candid note on who funded or sold them.
5. **The distinction.** Say outright which parts are performance and which are change.

Sentence-case H2s. Short sections. It is fine for one section to be two sentences.

## Habits

- **Ask who loses something.** If nobody does, it probably will not change behavior.
- **Say the uncomfortable thing once, clearly.** Then move on. Repeating it is sulking.
- **Be fair about motive.** "Nobody's career suffers from launching a training program" explains the behavior without accusing anyone.
- **Prefer the practical test.** "Before you buy the system, ask what decision it is supposed to change."
- **Allow dry humor.** A little goes far, and it should never outrun the evidence.

## Avoid

- Contempt for the people involved.
- Claiming certainty about motives you cannot see.
- Ending on a moral. End on the test someone can apply on Monday.

## Example (invented organization)

# The Board Dashboard Nobody Will Use

Our board is about to approve a dashboard. Before it does, someone should ask what the dashboard is for.

## What it does

The proposed dashboard tracks page views, newsletter opens, and event attendance. Each number is easy to pull and easy to show on a screen, which is exactly why it got approved. Nobody loses anything by launching a dashboard.

## What's missing

No one on the list has to decide anything differently because of it. There's no budget line that moves when the numbers do, and no person whose job changes. A dashboard that changes no decision is a decoration with a login.

I've seen organizations build elaborate ones that every board member opens once and no board member needs. The tool tends to measure what is cheap to count and not what the organization is trying to change.

## What the evidence says

Most of the research on this comes from software vendors, and it measures logins, not decisions. I'd treat it the way I'd treat a restaurant review written by the chef.

## The distinction

A dashboard is real if it can make someone give something up: a program that gets cut when it misses, a campaign that moves money when it works. If no number on it can do that, it's reporting, and reporting is fine, but call it that.

The test for Monday: pick one metric and write down what the board will do if it falls by a quarter. If nobody can answer, that metric doesn't belong on the screen.
