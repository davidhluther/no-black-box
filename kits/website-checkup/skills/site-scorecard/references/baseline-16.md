# The 16-item marketing baseline

Sixteen things every small business should have in place before it spends on ads or content. Nearly all of them cost time rather than money. The two small purchases are a web address and a phone number you can keep. The items fall into four phases. Mark each one Done, Needs done, or Not applicable, and note where you looked.

A short note on a divide that matters. Some businesses have a public door (a storefront). Others work without one (service calls, appointments, online). Where the answer differs, the item says how.

## Phase 1: Foundations (own your ground)

Nothing here is visible to a customer, and every later item breaks quietly when one of these is wrong. Each is also about ownership. The accounts and addresses belong to the business, not to a vendor or whoever set things up.

1. **One web address, everywhere.** To a computer, "www.example.org" and "example.org" are two different websites. Pick one, forward the other permanently, and make the site's pages, sitemap, and listings agree. Check: type both versions into a browser and see that one forwards to the other.
2. **A phone number you can keep forever.** The number spreads to every listing. Choose it deliberately, in a form you own and can carry anywhere. Changing it later means chasing it across everything you built. Check: ask whether the number belongs to the business or to a person or provider who could take it away.
3. **Email on your own domain that actually delivers.** Mail from you@example.org looks like a business. Three settings in the web address's records (called SPF, DKIM, and DMARC) tell the world your mail is yours. Without them, invoices and replies land in spam. Check: send a test to a free email account and see whether it arrives in the inbox.
4. **A written inventory of who owns what.** The registrar (where the address was bought), the DNS (the address book that says where the address points), hosting, the Google listing, social accounts, analytics. The owner holds every login or a recovery path, written down. Check: ask for the list.

## Phase 2: Presence (be findable)

The places people and computers check to decide you are real. The job here is accuracy and consistency, not posting.

5. **Google Business Profile.** Exact name, true category, honest hours, and a description that follows Google's rules. Storefront: publish the address and real hours. No public door: verify with a hidden address, declare the service area, and set appointment-required.
6. **Social profiles, claimed and consistent.** A profile is a public record that vouches for your identity. Claiming takes minutes. Posting is a separate decision for later.
7. **Listings beyond Google.** Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, Yelp, and the local chamber directory. The same name, number, and address, character for character. Help for businesses with no public address varies by platform and changes, so read each platform's current help page when you claim, and skip a platform that demands a street address you don't have.
8. **Real photos, no stock.** Stock photos read as the national chain you compete against. Storefront: the front, the inside, the people. No public door: you, the work itself, and the places it happens.
9. **A messaging kit.** The same positioning, pre-written at each platform's character limits, with images at each platform's sizes, so every profile says the same thing.

## Phase 3: Paths in (be reachable)

Both items fail silently. Nobody calls to say your form is down.

10. **Forms that deliver.** Send a test through every form on the site every month and confirm it arrives. A form can look live and deliver nothing.
11. **A booking path.** A way onto the calendar without phone tag, for the customer who decides at 9 p.m. For a business with no walk-in door this item counts double, because the booking path is the door.

## Phase 4: Instruments (know what's happening)

An installed instrument counts only once someone reads it.

12. **Search and visit measurement.** Search Console (the report card for Google), Bing Webmaster Tools, and a visit counter such as Google Analytics or Microsoft Clarity. Only Search Console tells you whether Google has filed your pages.
13. **AI-answer visibility.** Ask an AI model what your business does, and read the answer the way a stranger would. Note anything wrong or missing.
14. **A review flow.** The ask, the link, and an answer to every review.
15. **Backlink awareness.** Links from other sites are votes that search engines count, and spam links can count against you. Search Console's Links report shows who links to you for free. A formal disavow (telling Google to ignore certain links) is rare medicine for a real infestation.
16. **A monthly reading habit.** Fifteen minutes, three questions, written down in advance. Did anything break? What did search send? Where did people struggle? Checking more often produces mood swings. Checking less often is flying blind.

## Four small items without numbers

A favicon (the tiny icon in the browser tab), share images (the picture that shows when a link is posted), a web manifest (a small file that describes the site to phones), and HTTPS (the padlock). Whoever maintains the site can finish all four in an afternoon. The check is that they exist.
