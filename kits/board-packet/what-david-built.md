# What David Built

This page tells the story behind the board packet kit in plain terms, for anyone deciding whether David's way of working fits their organization.

## The problem

A small rural nonprofit's board met every month. Before each meeting, the secretary had to collect reports from several staff, chase the late ones, turn a recording of the last meeting into minutes, convert everything to PDF, combine it in the right order, upload it to the right folders, and write an email that linked it all. It lived in one volunteer's head and inbox. If that person was away, the packet was late. Minutes were written from memory and a recording nobody wanted to replay.

The board also wanted a record complete enough to answer questions long after the fact, and minutes short enough that people would actually read them. Those goals pull against each other.

## What David made

David Luther served as that board's secretary and turned the monthly scramble into a repeatable cycle. He wrote down the standing rules, such as which reports are expected, where minutes go, and what goes in the packet versus its own link. He then built tools around those rules.

- A monthly checklist and a Drive layout that keep the shared packet separate from the private recordings.
- A way to turn a meeting [transcript](glossary.md#transcript) into minutes in a consistent house style, with every motion recorded exactly and every unclear point flagged instead of guessed.
- A [digest](glossary.md#digest) of each meeting that keeps the figures, deadlines, and promises the minutes leave out, so a later question has an answer.
- A tool that converts, orders, and merges the packet, and lists any report that did not arrive so the email can say so honestly.
- A board email that is always written as a draft for a second person to read, never sent automatically.

For this public kit David rebuilt it for nonprofits that cannot run a [command line](glossary.md#command-line). The free version needs only Google Workspace and a free [AI model](glossary.md#ai-model). A built-in [check-up](glossary.md#check-up) prints a [support code](glossary.md#support-code), so a volunteer can ask for help without sharing a single name, address, or file. Middle and top versions add menus and automation for those who want them.

## What it changed

The monthly packet stopped depending on one person's memory. Late or missing reports are named in the email, not discovered at the meeting. Minutes are written from the transcript and checked against it, so a vote or a figure is not lost. The facts that fall out of the minutes now live in a digest anyone can find. The secretary's evenings got shorter, and the board got a record it can trust.

## Skills this shows

- Turning a messy recurring process into a written, repeatable workflow with clear roles, a master list, and a place for every file.
- Designing for the actual reader, a nontechnical volunteer, with plain language, defined terms, and steps that say how you will know each one worked.
- [Prompt](glossary.md#prompt) design for accuracy, where the model is told not to invent, uncertain points are marked, and a separate checking pass catches what the first pass missed.
- Building safe automation, such as scripts that only create drafts, only read the sheet, and say in their first lines what they will and will not do.
- Privacy by design, with recordings kept out of shared folders and a support report that carries no personal content.
- Writing for three audiences at once, a free tier anyone can follow, a mid tier for people who want menus, and a full tier for people who want an AI tool to run it all.
- Teaching, which is the point of the whole repository. Knowledge goes out free and complete, so a small organization does not have to pay someone to answer a simple question.

This kit is one of several in the same repository, each built from tools David uses in his own work.
