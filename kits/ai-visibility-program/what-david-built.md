# What David Built

This is the story behind the kit, written for a hiring manager or a team lead deciding whether the work is worth trusting.

## The problem

A B2B marketing team was asked, with growing urgency, whether AI search was changing how buyers found them. The tools on offer each gave a number, and the numbers did not agree. Search reports showed only what people typed into a search engine, so a question put to an AI model left no trace. The platforms sold to fill that gap tended to be black boxes: a score, a share of voice, little about which questions were asked or how a brand was matched. A team could not tell a real change from the model answering differently that day, and could not tell its own brand from a look-alike with the same name. When leaders asked "how do we know", the honest answer was that nobody could say.

## What David built

David Luther ran this work inside Corpay, a large business payments company, and turned the method into a program a team can operate.

- A fixed battery of buyer-style questions asked of several AI products on the same terms every month. The questions are frozen, so the instrument cannot move underneath the measurement.
- A same-name check done before the first run, so a look-alike is never counted as the company, and a clean-versus-raw count so every removal can be inspected.
- A refusal built into the tooling to compare runs that used different questions or different collection methods.
- A scheduled run across many sites and markets that records every attempt, retries a failure, and flags a missed month the same day, because a silent failure looks exactly like a quiet month.
- A rule that every source (the question battery, search data, web analytics and any paid platform) is shown side by side, never blended into one score, and that disagreement is explained before anyone recommends anything.
- A monthly report for leaders where every number traces to a file, the noise level is stated, and the data's blind spots are named.

## What it changed

The conversation moved from "what is our score" to "which questions, which answers, and how do we know". Disagreement between tools became a useful input instead of an embarrassment, because the first step was always to list the reasons they might differ. The team stopped treating a one-month wobble as a trend, and fixes to listings and pages were checked afterward to confirm they went live before anyone counted them. Ownership became explicit: one owner, a backup and a reviewer per site.

## Skills it shows

- Program design. Owners, cadence and handoffs for a measurement program that outlives the person who built it.
- Measurement discipline. Freezing an instrument, separating methods that must not be compared, and writing down what a number cannot say.
- Automation with accountability. Scheduled jobs that record, retry and report their own failures.
- AI-search strategy. Reading AI answers directly, tracing citations to sources, and turning findings into specific site and listing changes.
- Skepticism toward vendor metrics, and the plain-language writing to explain all of it to executives.

## His point of view

Much of this field is held in a black box, partly because it is complicated and partly because there is money in selling the answer back to you. David's view is that a team should be able to see how its numbers were made and decide for itself what to trust. The kit is that view in working form. If a team wants it fitted to its own platforms and reporting, he and Pigasus Group can do that, and the knowledge in the kit stays free.
