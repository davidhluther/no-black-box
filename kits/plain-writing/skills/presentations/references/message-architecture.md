# Message Architecture

## What is a Message?

A Message (capital M) is a packet of information you want to install in another person's brain to make them **think, feel, believe, and ultimately remember** something. It is not the words you say — it is the effect those words have after you leave the room.

## The Think / Feel / Believe Framework

Every Message must operate on three levels:

- **Think** → Provide logic, data, and facts. This is the rational case.
- **Feel** → Appeal to emotion — excitement about upside, or worry about consequences of inaction. Emotion drives action; logic justifies it afterward.
- **Believe** → Provide proof that is credible and sufficient for that specific person. Belief is the target. Memory is the byproduct.

## What a Message Is NOT

- **Not "delivered" until received.** Success is not measured by how many times you say it, but by whether the audience accurately received it. The test is what they think/feel/believe after you leave.
- **Not based on "rightness."** It doesn't matter if you believe the message is correct. Only what the receiver believes matters.
- **Not the same as the words you use.** Overusing words can install the opposite message. Repeatedly saying "there is no risk" installs the message "there's risk here." The message is the effect, not the words.

## The 8-Step Preparation Process

Slide creation is step 6. Not step 1.

1. **Define the Outcome** — What do you want to achieve?
   - Consider where the audience is in the buying/decision cycle
   - Have an ideal outcome AND a minimally acceptable outcome
   - Make the outcome valuable to the audience, not just to you
   - Be achievable but stretching — move the process forward meaningfully

2. **Assess the People** — Who is in the room?
   - Formal vs. informal influence (title ≠ real power)
   - Business agenda: How are they measured? What are their KPIs?
   - Personal agenda: What do they want? Visibility? An easy life? Low risk?
   - Style and pre-existing perceptions of you and your company

3. **Define the Messages** — 3 to 5 Messages maximum.
   - Each must positively differentiate you from alternatives (including doing nothing)
   - Each should map to a specific person or stakeholder group
   - For each: define the Think target, Feel target, and Believe target

4. **Determine the Flow** — Choose the structural model (see flow-models.md)

5. **Expand Detail** — Brainstorm specific content for each section
   - What data supports Think?
   - What emotional hooks support Feel?
   - What proof points support Believe?
   - Design high-impact open-ended questions (TEDY: Tell me, Explain, Describe, Your view on...)

6. **Define/Create Collateral** — NOW you make slides.
   - Match the environment (formality, audience size)
   - Synchronize visuals with spoken words — never show something that conflicts with what you're saying
   - Use collateral to deliver proof points

7. **Practice** — Rehearse the opening 60 seconds and closing 90 seconds verbatim. Check hand-offs for team presentations.

8. **Prepare the Customer** — Set expectations before the meeting.

## Message Mapping Template

For each Message, complete this:

```
Message [N] — Target: [Person/Role]
Goal: [One sentence describing what this message achieves]
Think: [What rational conclusion should they reach?]
Feel: [What emotion should they experience?]
Believe: [What proof will make this credible to THIS person?]
Proof type: [Mini-case / data / reference / operational artifact / pilot offer]
```

## Three Default Messages for Capabilities Presentations

When planning a capabilities or approach presentation, start with these three and customize:

1. **Relevance:** "We understand your world and your stakes."
2. **Credibility:** "We've solved adjacent problems with measurable outcomes."
3. **Approach:** "Here's a low-risk, staged way we work — respecting your time."
