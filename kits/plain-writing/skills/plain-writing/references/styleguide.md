# Plain Writing Style Guide

This is the full rule set behind the plain-writing skill. It is a baseline for any prose a person will read: web pages, newsletters, grant text, board memos, emails, posts, reports. An organization can add a stricter house style on top (see the last section). Nothing in a house style may loosen this baseline.

**Sources.** Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" page, plus a 2026 study of narrative structure called StoryScope (arXiv 2604.03136), plus editing rules that kept coming up in real review rounds. Read the original sources for their numbers; this guide uses them for direction only.

**Re-check every quarter.** Tells change as models change. A word that was a giveaway last year may be gone, and the next one is already forming. Read the current Wikipedia page again each quarter and add what you see to your word list.

## Why these rules exist

The goal is prose that reads as if a person who knows the subject wrote it, and that a reader can trust. It is not a promise about search rankings or about fooling a detector. Three consequences follow.

1. **Language tells block. Formatting density only warns.** Banned vocabulary, filler openers, and hollow parallelism are errors. Em-dash counts, colon counts, and bold counts are formatting measures. Treat them as prompts to look again, never as grounds for stripping headings, lists, tables, or bold from a page that has them.
2. **Fix the prose, keep the scaffolding.** Hitting a formatting count by deleting structure makes the page harder to read. Rewrite sentences instead.
3. **Do not read the rules backwards.** Avoiding these tells does not make writing good, and a clean score does not prove a person wrote it. The rules remove distractions so the reader can judge the substance.

Depth is a goal of its own. Develop a topic until it is actually sufficient, then stop. Padding to hit a word count brings back every tell below.

## Tier 1: hard bans (flag every instance)

Any occurrence is an error. Remove or rewrite it before the piece goes out.

### Vocabulary

Words that are tells wherever they appear. Replace each with a plain alternative.

- delve, delving, delved
- tapestry
- realm
- beacon
- cacophony
- multifaceted
- commendable
- paramount
- commence (say "start" or "begin")
- utilize (say "use")
- facilitate (name what actually happens)
- harness (say "use" or name the mechanism)
- illuminate, when used as a metaphor (say "show" or "explain")
- testament, as in "a testament to"
- underscore, as a verb (say "emphasize" or delete it)
- foster, when metaphorical (say what actually happens)
- leveraging, leverages, as jargon verbs (say "use"). The noun, as in bargaining leverage, stays fine.
- seamless, seamlessly (describe the real experience)
- streamline (say what gets faster or simpler)
- empower (say what the person can now do)
- unlock (say what becomes available)
- elevate (say what improves and how)

### Opener phrases

Chat-assistant habits. Never open a piece of writing with these.

- "Certainly!"
- "Great question!"
- "I'd be happy to"
- "Let's dive in"
- "Let's explore" or "Let's break it down"
- "Here's the thing:"
- "Think of it as..." or "Imagine a world where..."
- "Whether you're a [X] or a [Y]"

### Formulaic transitions

Connectors that signal a machine. Use a natural transition, or start the next thought.

- Moreover
- Furthermore
- In addition
- On the other hand
- That said
- With that in mind
- It goes without saying
- Needless to say
- At the end of the day

### Meta-commentary

Writing about the writing. Delete it. The reader does not need a map of what they are already reading.

- "In this article, we will..."
- "This guide walks through..."
- "We'll examine..." or "In what follows..."
- "This article will help you..."
- "By the end of this piece, you'll understand..."
- "The sections below cover..."
- "As we've discussed..."
- "It's important to note that..." or "It is worth noting that..."
- "No discussion would be complete without..."

If the introduction needs to set expectations, state a thesis or name a specific question the reader has. Do not describe the document's own structure.

### Time clichés

Generic stage-setting. Name the specific change instead.

- "In today's ever-evolving world"
- "In today's fast-paced landscape"
- "In an increasingly digital world"
- "In today's business environment"
- "In our rapidly changing world"

### Summary openers and closers

Banned as the end of a section or piece. If the content was clear, it needs no recap. A navigational recap is acceptable only in a long report (over about 1,500 words).

- "In summary," "In conclusion," "In essence"
- "Overall," "To sum up"
- "The bottom line is"
- Any closing paragraph that restates what the section just said

### Placeholder examples

Never write "One logistics company...", "Take a mid-sized manufacturer...", or "Consider a growing business...". Either use a real, named example with real context, or describe the situation directly without an invented actor. (If you must invent an example for teaching, label it as invented.)

### Repeated statistics

State each fact or figure once, where it matters most. A later reference can be brief ("that 70 percent drop") but never restates the full figure.

## Tier 2: overuse watches (flag if more than one per 500 words)

One use is fine. Repetition in a short span is the tell.

### Inflation adjectives

If removing the word does not change the meaning, remove it.

key, crucial, vital, pivotal, significant, noteworthy, essential, robust, vibrant, dynamic, cutting-edge, revolutionary, innovative

### Copula replacements

Fancy stand-ins for plain "is."

- "stands as" (use "is")
- "serves as" (use "is" or say what it does)
- "represents," used metaphorically (use "is")
- "functions as" (use "is" or "acts as")

### Verbs and metaphors

- "align with" (say what the two things actually share)
- "ensure" (say "make sure" or describe the mechanism)
- "navigate," used metaphorically (say "manage," "handle," "work through")
- "landscape," used metaphorically (say "market," "field," or name the thing)
- "comprehensive" (say what it covers)

## Tier 3: structural patterns

These are detectable by shape, not vocabulary. Each is allowed rarely. Overuse, or using one in place of a specific detail, is the tell.

### Negative parallelism

At most one per piece, and never the piece's signature move.

- "not just X, but Y"
- "It's not X, it's Y" or "It's about X, not Y"
- "more than just X, it's Y"
- "X doesn't just Y; it Z"
- The cross-sentence version: "The problem isn't X. It's Y." Same move split across two sentences, and it counts toward the same limit of one.

### Rule of three

Stacked near-synonyms are padding ("fast, efficient, and streamlined" is one idea in three coats). Three genuinely different items are fine ("check, ACH, and virtual card" are three payment methods). Do not default to exactly three examples. Use as many as the point needs.

### False ranges

Use "from X to Y" only for real, continuous ranges ("from 2020 to 2024"). "From volunteers to board members to donors" is a list pretending to be a spectrum.

### Participle tails

Sentence-final "-ing" clauses that add vague importance: "...emphasizing its significance," "...underscoring the importance," "...solidifying its reputation." Cut them.

### Promotional tail phrases

"plays a significant role," "continues to captivate," "leaves a lasting impact," "a testament to," "watershed moment," "deeply rooted in," "rich history." Replace with a specific or delete.

### Sentence-shape tells

- **The "This is where..." pivot.** "This is where the pain hits hardest." Rewrite without it.
- **Adverb-stacked gerunds.** "Significantly shorter cycles, dramatically lower costs, and a substantial boost." Cut the intensifiers and pick the strongest verb.
- **Mid-piece wrap-ups.** Sentences that summarize halfway through an argument and reset the pace. Cut them.
- **The "Actually" tic.** "What X Actually Does" in headings, repeated across subheads. One per piece, and only to contrast a stated assumption.
- **Anaphora runs.** Three or more sentences in a row that start with the same word, especially "This" or "It." Break the run by the second sentence.

### Heading case

The title of a piece is in title case. Every H2 and H3 below it is in sentence case: first word and proper nouns only. Models drift back to title case in subheadings without noticing, so treat this as a final pass, not a preference.

### Uniform sentence length

Five or more sentences in a row within about three words of the same length read as assembled. Mix short sentences with longer ones. Do not over-fragment either. Related ideas usually belong in one sentence, and short declaratives are for emphasis.

## Tier 4: mechanics and limits

| Item | Limit |
|---|---|
| Em-dashes in body prose | About one per 200 words, with spaces on both sides. Prefer commas or periods. Formatting measure: warn only. |
| Sentence-internal colons | Two or three per piece. First choice is no colon at all. Formatting measure: warn only. |
| Semicolons | About one per 500 words. Join only two closely related complete clauses. Most read better as two sentences. |
| Paragraph length | Six sentences at most; aim for five. |
| Rhetorical comma lists (four or more short items) | Two per piece at most. A factual list of proper nouns is exempt. |
| Bold spans | Eight per piece when drafting new prose. Do not bold the lead of every list item or every topic sentence. When fixing an existing page, keep its bold. |
| Same-opener sentence runs | Never three in a row. |

### Colon discipline

The order of preference.

1. **Reword or use a comma.** Most colons are a small rewrite from not existing. The "...a plan: item, item, item" skeleton is a machine fingerprint.
2. **Keep a colon only when it earns its place.** A true list, a label, or a setup and payoff the structure demands. If it reads fine without one, it goes.
3. **When you keep one, use AP capitalization.** Capitalize the first word after the colon if a complete sentence follows (including a command). Keep lowercase for fragments and lists.

Examples.

- Instead of "The budget has one problem: the revenue assumptions are stale," write "The budget's one real problem is stale revenue assumptions."
- Instead of "The takeaway is clear: apply early," write "The clear takeaway is to apply early." If you want the emphasis, capitalize: "The takeaway is clear: Apply early."
- This one earns its colon, and the list stays lowercase: "Bring three documents: the budget, the audit, and the enrollment table."

### Mechanics that always apply

- Straight quotes only, never curly.
- No emojis in prose.
- Contractions are fine. That is how people write.
- Oxford comma.
- No hyphen after an adverb ("widely used platform").
- No inline lettered lists like "(a) speed, (b) accuracy." Use prose or a real list.
- Active voice by default. Use the passive only when the actor is unknown or does not matter.
- Links sit inside the sentence's natural thought, with descriptive anchor text. No "click here," and no "For more on X, see [link]" callouts.
- A series always closes with a conjunction: "tenure, staff, and stations he owned," never "tenure, staff, stations he owned."
- No decorative arrows on links. A link is a link. An arrow is fine only when it points at something on the same page.
- A heading with a label separator uses a colon, not an em-dash ("Step 1: Receive the invoice").
- A bold lead-in label ends with a period, never an em-dash. Write "**Issuance.** The system generates...", not "**Issuance** — the system...". Drop the label if the sentence stands alone.
- Never use an em-dash to introduce a series. Reword, or use a colon with AP capitalization.
- Separate side-by-side facts with a vertical bar (|), never a middle dot.

### Numbers and dates

The kit default is AP style with one choice made for you: spell out zero through ten and use digits from 11 up. (Strict AP spells out only zero through nine. Your house style can switch.) Never start a sentence with a numeral. Spell the number out or rewrite. Keep digits for money, percentages, ages, ratings, table cells, and arithmetic. Write dollar amounts in full in prose ($27,000, not $27k). Pick one date format for the whole organization and never mix formats in one document.

### Rendered-output mechanics

Rules only hold in the version the reader receives. A web template can break them without any sign in the draft. For example, in some JSX builds a text element that contains an HTML entity and wraps across source lines loses the space before it, so "range is scored" ships as "rangeis scored." Metadata titles, link labels, navigation labels, and table cells are copy too, and a draft-stage check never sees them. Check the finished page, not just the draft. The copy-lint skill does this.

### Terminology

Say **AI models**, never "AI assistants." Named products keep their names: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Perplexity are products, and "AI models" is the category term. When a sentence is about a chat window or an app rather than the model, name the app.

## Tier 5: structural authenticity

Vocabulary checks catch last year's tells. The structure of the whole piece is the durable fingerprint, and the StoryScope work reports that editing the surface barely changed whether a text was detected. So these are planning rules. Fix them in the outline, not in a polish pass.

- **Do not state the lesson.** Make the point through the material, then stop. No closing sentence that tells the reader what they just learned.
- **Do not resolve everything.** Real writing leaves a loose end, an open question, or a stated unknown. If every section ends settled, unsettle one.
- **Name real things.** Specific texts, brands, places, and people beat vague allusion. "The Maple Street light" beats "local traffic," and a named study beats "research shows."
- **Address the reader when it is natural.** An aside to the reader is a human signature.
- **Say a feeling plainly sometimes.** Models perform emotion through stacked bodily metaphors. If every reaction is a tightening chest, name one plainly: it was frustrating.
- **Take a position.** Commit to specific stances instead of converging on the safe middle. Real ambivalence is fine where it is honest.
- **Vary the energy.** Let one section be short and sharp and another discursive. A digression that earns its place is a human tell.
- **Know your own tool's fingerprint.** Each model has a tendency. A common one is evenness: flat escalation, one uniform voice, quiet tidy endings. Break register somewhere on purpose.

## Craft baseline

- **Reason from mechanism, not authority.** Explain why X follows. Cite a study to support your reasoning, not to replace it.
- **Every statistic gets a named, checkable source** ("according to [the named report, its publisher, and its year]"), never "studies show" or "experts say." If you cannot verify a number, describe the trend in words. Never invent a source, and never keep a doubtful number inside a hedge.
- **Depth over breadth.** Two or three developed examples beat ten name-drops. A pile of citations reads like database retrieval.
- **Vary section rhythm.** Not every section the same length or shape. Some unevenness is a feature.
- **One authored moment minimum.** Every substantial piece includes at least one observation specific enough that a reader in the audience thinks "this person has actually done this." Generic advice ("do your due diligence") does not count.
- **Use conversational qualifiers where they are honest:** "probably," "as far as I can tell," "anecdotally." Signal real uncertainty, not performed humility. Question doubtful numbers instead of hedging around them.

## Displacement watch

Banning a tell does not remove the urge that produced it. Drafting reaches for the nearest form no rule names. When the in-sentence "not just X, but Y" is banned, the two-sentence "The problem isn't X. It's Y." grows to fill the gap. Semicolons can stand in for banned colons and dashes.

1. **Ban the move, then list its known siblings in the same edit.** Write the rule at the level of the move ("negative parallelism in any arrangement"), then list the forms.
2. **After any rule change, compare before and after.** The tell-scorer skill's cohort comparison shows which rows got worse next to the ones that improved. An improved row beside a worsened neighbor is the signature of displacement.
3. **Each quarter, sweep for new tics.** The tell-scorer skill's emergent-tic script lists words and phrases that rose in new writing against an older baseline. Read the list. Do not auto-ban it, because some of the rise is new subject matter and some is a new habit.

## House style: the organization layer

A house style is one short file that holds everything particular to one organization: its name and product names, its voice, preferred spellings, number and date conventions, extra banned words, extra required checks. It sits on top of this baseline.

- **Stricter only.** A house style can add rules, tighten limits, or ban more words. It cannot allow anything this guide bans.
- **One file per organization.** A volunteer group, a school, and a shop each get their own. The shared baseline stays untouched, so an improvement here reaches every house style at once.
- **Where it lives.** Point to the file with the `house_style_file` key in your `config.json`, or paste its contents into your AI Project's instructions. The skill reads it after the baseline and says which rule came from which layer.
- **How to start.** Copy `house-style-template.md` from this folder, fill in what you know, and delete the rest. Three lines is a fine house style.
- **Word lists.** Extra banned words go in your copy of `data/style_rules.json`. The article-check skill reads that file.

Keep the house style short. Every rule in it should come from something that went wrong once and cost a round of review.
