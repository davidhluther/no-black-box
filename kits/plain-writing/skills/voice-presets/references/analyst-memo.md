# Analyst Memo

A rigorous, mechanism-first voice for articles, board memos, and reports. It diagnoses the real problem, explains how it works, and ends on specific recommendations.

## The brief

Write with analytical rigor and strategic clarity, grounded in research and evidence. Look for the underlying mechanism and who holds the power to change it, rather than a surface fix. Separate performative approaches from substantive ones. Use concrete examples and natural experiments to illustrate each point. Keep a skeptical but constructive tone that admits complexity and still pushes toward something to do. Build the argument in this order: the problem as it first appears, the real problem underneath, how it works, what to do.

## Shape

1. **Title and short opening.** Title case for the title. One paragraph that states what looks like the problem and why that reading is incomplete.
2. **Diagnosis.** What the numbers show, and what they miss. Name the measure that looks good and does not tell you about outcomes.
3. **Mechanism.** The step-by-step reason the real problem exists. This is the heart of the memo and gets the most space.
4. **Recommendations.** Specific, ordered, each tied to the mechanism. Say what each one costs someone, because the changes that work usually take something from someone.
5. **What we don't know.** One short section that names a gap and the cheapest way to close it.

Use sentence-case H2 headings. Two to five of them. Never a "Conclusion" or "Summary" section.

## Habits

- **Distinguish activity from outcome.** Training hours, meetings held, and posts published measure effort. Ask what changed for the people the work is for.
- **Use a small example from inside the organization.** A specific quarter, program, or decision does more than a general principle.
- **Acknowledge cost.** "That is uncomfortable because it takes discretion away from managers" is stronger than a recommendation that costs no one anything.
- **Cite to support the reasoning.** Name the source, its date, and say what it does and doesn't show.

## Avoid

- Recommendation lists with no mechanism behind them.
- "Key takeaways" boxes and recap paragraphs.
- Stacked triplets of near-synonyms ("clear, concise, and compelling").

## Example (invented organization and figures)

# Why Our Volunteer Program Looks Healthy and Isn't

The quarterly report says the program is thriving. I think it is measuring the wrong thing, and the board should know before it renews the coordinator's budget.

## What the report shows

Last quarter's volunteer report counted 340 hours logged, a 90 percent satisfaction rating, and 12 new sign-ups. All three numbers are good. None of them says whether the shelves were stocked on Saturday morning.

## The mechanism

Hours are logged by whoever shows up, and shifts that nobody takes are not logged at all. So the total can rise while the number of uncovered Saturday openings rises too. We saw this in March, when two long-time volunteers added hours on weekdays and the Saturday crew shrank from six to four. The report went up, and the experience at the door got worse.

The satisfaction rating has the same blind spot, because it comes from a survey sent to people who completed a shift, so anyone who stopped coming never answers it.

## What would change this

We should count open shifts as well as filled hours, and report the Saturday morning crew size every week. That is uncomfortable for one reason: it shows the coordinator's own schedule gaps. We should also call the five people who stopped signing up in the last six months and ask why. I'd rather hear the answers than guess them.

## What we don't know

We haven't checked whether Saturday is the real constraint or just the one we notice. A month of weekly counts would settle it.
